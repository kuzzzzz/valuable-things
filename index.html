10 Valuable Things

Why they matter and how their value was created over time — from ancient scarcity to modern markets.

Gold Diamonds Oil Bitcoin Spices Fine Art Fresh Water Knowledge Land Semiconductors
01

Gold

Why it is valuable

Gold is scarce, durable, divisible, portable, and universally recognized. It does not corrode, cannot be printed, and has served as money and a store of value for more than 5,000 years. Its value comes from trust in its physical properties rather than any government’s promise.

Timeline of value

c. 4500 BCE
Earliest worked gold found in the Varna necropolis (Bulgaria). Already treated as a prestige material.
c. 2500 BCE
Egyptian and Nubian mining at scale. Gold becomes the metal of gods, kings, and tombs.
c. 550 BCE
King Croesus of Lydia issues the first standardized pure gold coins — the birth of gold as formal money.
1816–1873
Britain and then much of the world adopt the classical gold standard. Gold underpins global trade and currencies.
1933–1971
U.S. revalues gold to $35/oz and locks private ownership; Bretton Woods pegs the dollar to gold.
1971 – present
Nixon ends dollar convertibility. Gold becomes a free-floating asset and modern hedge against inflation and geopolitical risk. Prices later reach multi-thousand-dollar highs.
02

Diamonds

Why it is valuable

Natural diamonds are hard, beautiful, and relatively scarce in gem quality. Much of their modern price, however, was deliberately engineered: a cartel controlled supply while a brilliant marketing campaign linked diamonds to eternal love and social status, turning a commodity into an emotional necessity.

Timeline of value

Ancient India
Diamonds known and prized for centuries, mainly among royalty and the wealthy.
1860s–1888
Massive diamond discoveries in South Africa. Cecil Rhodes consolidates mines into De Beers, which eventually controls ~90% of global supply and creates artificial scarcity.
1938–1948
De Beers hires N.W. Ayer. The agency invents the modern diamond engagement ring tradition and launches the slogan “A Diamond Is Forever.”
1940s–1980s
Aggressive U.S. and later global campaigns make diamond rings a social norm. U.S. wholesale diamond sales explode from tens of millions to billions of dollars.
2000s–present
Lab-grown diamonds and new mine sources challenge the old monopoly, yet natural diamonds retain strong emotional and investment value for many buyers.
03

Crude Oil

Why it is valuable

Oil is the densest widely available energy source that powered the 20th century. It fuels transport, industry, plastics, fertilizers, and chemicals. Control of oil has shaped geopolitics, wars, and national wealth for more than a century.

Timeline of value

Ancient times
Bitumen and surface oil used for waterproofing, medicine, and construction in Mesopotamia and elsewhere.
1859
Edwin Drake drills the first commercial oil well in Titusville, Pennsylvania. The modern petroleum industry is born.
1885–1920s
Internal-combustion engines and mass automobile production turn gasoline from a waste product into the primary driver of demand.
1938–1960
Major Middle Eastern discoveries (Saudi Arabia etc.). OPEC is founded in 1960 as producers begin asserting control over prices.
1973–1979
Oil embargos and the Iranian Revolution trigger price shocks that reshape the global economy and energy policy.
2000s–present
Shale revolution, OPEC+, energy transition debates. Oil remains central to transport and petrochemicals even as renewables grow.
04

Bitcoin

Why it is valuable

Bitcoin is the first successful digital scarcity. Its fixed supply of 21 million coins, decentralized issuance, and resistance to censorship create a form of “digital gold.” Value arises from network effects, scarcity, and growing institutional and sovereign demand.

Timeline of value

2008
Satoshi Nakamoto publishes the Bitcoin white paper describing peer-to-peer electronic cash without trusted intermediaries.
2009
Genesis block mined. First known price: fractions of a cent. Network is tiny and experimental.
2010–2013
Pizza Day (10,000 BTC for two pizzas). First major bull run takes Bitcoin past $1,000 amid growing media attention.
2017 & 2021
Retail and later institutional waves push prices to successive all-time highs (~$20k then ~$69k). ETFs and corporate treasuries begin to appear.
2024–2025
U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs approved. Fourth halving reduces new supply. Price reaches new highs above $100,000 as adoption deepens.
05

Spices (esp. Pepper, Cloves, Cinnamon)

Why they were (and sometimes still are) valuable

In the pre-modern world, spices preserved food, flavored bland diets, served as medicine, and signaled extreme wealth. Their value was driven by enormous transport costs, multiple middlemen, and deliberate scarcity. At times a pound of pepper cost more than a skilled craftsman’s several days’ wages; some spices approached the price of gold by weight.

Timeline of value

Ancient–Medieval
Arab, Indian, and Chinese traders move spices across the Indian Ocean and Silk Road. Alexandria and later Venice become European gateways.
12th–15th centuries
European demand surges after the Crusades. Spices become the most profitable long-distance trade goods. Venice dominates the European end of the trade.
1498–1600s
Portuguese and then Dutch break the Arab–Venetian monopoly by sailing around Africa. The Dutch East India Company builds a near-monopoly on cloves and nutmeg.
18th–19th centuries
Cultivation spreads, transport improves, and prices collapse. Spices become everyday commodities rather than luxury treasures.
Today
Most spices are inexpensive, but high-quality saffron, vanilla, and certain single-origin peppers still command premium prices.
06

Fine Art

Why it is valuable

Art combines scarcity (unique or limited works), cultural prestige, historical importance, and, increasingly, investment demand. Value is created by reputation of the artist, provenance, exhibition history, critical consensus, and the wealth of collectors competing for status and returns.

Timeline of value

Middle Ages – Renaissance
Value tied mainly to costly materials (gold leaf, lapis lazuli) and the patron’s status. Artists work under commission.
17th–19th centuries
Art market develops in the Netherlands and later Britain and France. Old Masters gain enduring reputations; auctions become important.
Late 19th – mid-20th century
Impressionists and modernists move from rejection to high prices. Artists themselves become brands.
1980s–present
Contemporary art explodes as a global asset class. Record auction prices, wealth concentration, and financialization turn top works into portable stores of value and status symbols.
07

Fresh Water

Why it is valuable

Water is essential for life, agriculture, industry, and energy. While abundant on Earth overall, clean, accessible freshwater is scarce in many regions. Climate change, population growth, and pollution are increasing its economic and strategic value.

Timeline of value

Ancient civilizations
Settlements cluster around reliable rivers and springs. Control of water equals power (Egypt, Mesopotamia, Indus Valley).
Industrial era
Large-scale irrigation, dams, and municipal water systems turn water into an engineered utility, often treated as nearly free.
Late 20th century
Growing recognition of scarcity, pollution, and aquifer depletion. Water markets and pricing reforms appear in some countries.
21st century
Climate stress, urbanization, and industrial demand push water higher on economic and geopolitical agendas. Desalination and efficiency technologies gain importance.
08

Knowledge & Education

Why it is valuable

Knowledge compounds. It raises productivity, enables better decisions, and creates new technologies and institutions. Unlike most physical goods, it can be shared without being depleted, yet high-quality, scarce expertise still commands premium prices in labor markets and innovation.

Timeline of value

Ancient world
Libraries (Alexandria), philosophical schools, and scribal training concentrate knowledge among elites.
15th century
Printing press dramatically lowers the cost of reproducing knowledge and accelerates the Renaissance and Scientific Revolution.
19th–20th centuries
Mass public education and research universities turn human capital into a central driver of economic growth.
1990s–present
Internet and digital tools make vast amounts of information free, while specialized skills, data, and AI training become the new scarce and highly paid forms of knowledge.
09

Land & Real Estate

Why it is valuable

Land is finite. Location, legal rights, and improvements (buildings, infrastructure) create value. In growing cities and productive agricultural regions, land has been one of the most reliable long-term stores of wealth across cultures and centuries.

Timeline of value

Agricultural revolution
Settled farming makes control of fertile land the foundation of wealth and power.
Feudal & early modern eras
Land ownership defines social hierarchy. Enclosure movements and colonial land grants concentrate property rights.
19th–20th centuries
Urbanization and industrialization make city land extremely valuable. Homeownership becomes a major middle-class wealth vehicle in many countries.
Present
Location still dominates: coastal, transit-rich, and high-amenity cities command large premiums. Zoning, climate risk, and remote work continue to reshape relative values.
10

Semiconductors (Silicon Chips)

Why they are valuable

Modern semiconductors are the physical substrate of computing, communications, AI, and almost every electronic device. Extreme technical complexity, enormous capital costs, and geopolitical concentration make advanced chips both economically critical and strategically scarce.

Timeline of value

1947–1950s
Invention of the transistor (Bell Labs) and the integrated circuit. Electronics begin shrinking and becoming reliable.
1960s–1980s
Moore’s Law takes hold. Silicon Valley and later East Asian foundries turn chips into a mass industry powering personal computers and consumer electronics.
1990s–2010s
Mobile phones, internet infrastructure, and data centers create enormous demand. Leading-edge fabrication becomes extraordinarily expensive.
2020s
AI boom, export controls, and national industrial policies elevate advanced semiconductors to the level of strategic national assets. Value is measured in both market price and geopolitical leverage.
